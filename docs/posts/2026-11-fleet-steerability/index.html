Steerability of a robot fleet by one overseer

Mirrored axioms and their relation to human power

Status: AI-assisted sketch

The definitions, statements and proofs in this note were drafted by Claude (Fable 5.2) in one conversation with me, starting from my questions. No human has checked the proofs yet. The inequalities were tested numerically on random finite instances (20,000 each for the attainment and power results, 3,000 each for the prevention results), and the worked examples were recomputed by the same scripts. The propositions that derive functional forms from axioms (prevention forms, aggregation without anonymity) are stated with proof sketches only.

Note: PDF, 14 pages · Source and test scripts: source.zip

Summary

Abel et al. (2025) call the influence of observations on an agent’s actions plasticity and define it by a directed-information quantity. The note asks what the axiomatic approach of Heitzig and Potham (2026) to human power gives for the mirrored question: how well can one overseer steer each of several robots with fixed policies? The standard steerability goal is to prevent a certain robot action. Goals to make a robot do something (attainment goals) are a variant, used to relate steerability to the overseer’s power over the world and to the robots’ own power.

Steerability axioms carry the labels of the corresponding power axioms with a prefixed “s”, for example (sI5) for Reliability Preference. Variants carry a prime, and so do all steerability parameters (\(\zeta'\), \(\xi'\), \(\kappa'\)).

Standard steerability: prevention goals

Let \(q(c)\) be the largest probability with which the overseer can keep robot \(r\) from showing the action or behaviour cell \(c\). For prevention, an action that cannot be prevented is the worst case and one that can be prevented with certainty is the harmless case. Two power axioms are therefore replaced: Unreachable Goal Invariance by Trivial Goal Invariance (sI4′: a goal with capability 1 is neutral), and Reliability Preference by its opposite, Weakest-Goal Preference (sI5′: moving capability from a more to a less attainable goal raises steerability). Composition additivity is then required for a single goal only (sI7′). The result is a one-parameter family,

\[S_r=-\zeta'\log_2\Big(1+\sum_{c}\big(q(c)^{-1/\zeta'}-1\big)\Big),\qquad \sum_c\log_2 q(c)\;\le\;S_r\;\le\;\log_2\min_c q(c),\]

with \(S_r\le 0\) and \(S_r=0\) exactly when every action can be prevented with certainty.

  • \(\zeta'=\infty\) gives the sum of the log prevention capabilities.
  • \(\zeta'\to0\) gives the minimum, \(\log_2\min_c q(c)\). This limit is only weakly monotone in each capability (sI2′ in place of sI2).
  • \(S_r\) is nonincreasing in \(\zeta'\).
  • Keeping Unreachable Goal Invariance instead gives \(\log_2\sum_c q(c)^{\zeta'}\) with \(0<\zeta'<1\), in which an unpreventable action is neutral and no minimum arises.
  • A robot that ignores the overseer has \(q(c)=1-\pi(c)\), where \(\pi(c)\) is the probability that it shows \(c\). Prevention steerability therefore counts an action as preventable also when the robot is unlikely to take it anyway: a deterministic robot that ignores the overseer has \(S_r=-\infty\), a uniformly random one has \(S_r\) near \(0\).

Trajectory goals and a greedy recursion

For sequences of prevention goals (“do not take \(a^1\) in step 1, \(a^2\) in step 2, …”) the sum over goals can be avoided. Let \(q(s,a_r)\) be the largest probability that the robot does not take \(a_r\) in the step after state \(s\), attained by the overseer action \(a_h(s,a_r)\), and let \(\tilde\sigma(s,a_r)\) be the resulting distribution of the successor state given that \(a_r\) is avoided. For an exponent \(e\), define

\[R_e(s)=1+\sum_{a_r}q(s,a_r)^e\sum_{s'}\tilde\sigma(s,a_r)(s')\,R_e(s'),\qquad R_e(s)=1\ \text{for terminal } s .\]

  • With \(e=-1/\zeta'\) and \(|\mathcal G(s)|\) the number of goals, \(-\zeta'\log_2\big(1+R_e(s)-|\mathcal G(s)|\big)\) is a lower bound on the standard steerability over all trajectory goals. It is exact for a greedy overseer if transitions are deterministic.
  • For \(\zeta'\to0\) the recursion becomes \(M(s)=\min_{a_r}q(s,a_r)\min_{s'}M(s')\), which adds up the log probabilities of preventing the least preventable action along a worst path.
  • The greedy overseer ignores later steps. On random small models it loses about 0.01 bits against the optimal overseer, and the recursion another 0.03 to 0.16 bits.
  • The same recursion with the unconditional successor distribution is close numerically but is not a bound in either direction.

Attainment goals and the null axiom

With \(p(g)\) the largest probability with which the overseer can bring about a behaviour goal \(g\), the unchanged power axioms (sI1–7) give the attainment steerability of robot \(r\) and the joint attainment steerability of the fleet \(\mathcal R\),

\[S^+_r=\log_2\sum_{g\in\mathcal G_r}p(g)^{\zeta'_+},\qquad S^+_{\mathcal R}=\log_2\sum_{g\in\mathcal G_{\mathcal R}}p(g)^{\zeta'_+},\qquad \zeta'_+>1 .\]

A null axiom (sN: all robots that ignore the overseer have the same steerability) contradicts Reliability Preference (sI5) and forces \(\zeta'_+=1\). For one-shot commands and singleton goals, the \(\zeta'_+=1\) measure is the maximal leakage of Issa et al. (2020). Prevention steerability violates the null axiom.

Fleet-level aggregation

  • Form. The renamed axioms (sP0–8) give \[F=-\tfrac1{\xi'}\log_2\sum_r 2^{-\xi' S_r},\qquad \min_r S_r-\tfrac{\log_2 n}{\xi'}\le F\le\min_r S_r .\]
  • Least-robot focus. Losing one bit of one robot’s steerability is uncompensable by \(k\) other robots if and only if \(k\le 2^{\xi'}-1\). Uncompensability for all fleet sizes forces \(F=\min_r S_r\) and gives up strict monotonicity.
  • Granularity. \(F\) is invariant under replacing a group of robots by a representative with their aggregate steerability. For attainment goals it is not invariant under merging robots into a coalition: that invariance would force \(F=S^+_{\mathcal R}\), which is a sum for independent robots and violates inequality aversion (sP6).
  • No such conflict for prevention goals. If a robot with \(S_r=0\) is neutral (sP4′) and \(\xi'=1/\zeta'\), the aggregate \(-\tfrac1{\xi'}\log_2\big(1+\sum_r(2^{-\xi' S_r}-1)\big)\) equals the steerability of one robot with all the fleet’s prevention goals. It is invariant under coalition merges and inequality-averse.
  • Without anonymity of robots, the aggregate becomes \(F=-\tfrac1{\xi'}\log_2\sum_r 2^{-\xi'(S_r-\omega_r)}\) with robot-specific offsets \(\omega_r\). Unweighted inequality aversion forces equal offsets. If the offset depends on a robot only through its power \(I_r\) (sP1′), single-robot composition additivity (sP8) gives \(\omega_r=\kappa' I_r\) with one new parameter \(\kappa'\ge0\).

Joint and individual attainment steerability

\(S^+_{\mathcal R}=\sum_r S^+_r\) for independently commanded robots, and \(S^+_{\mathcal R}\le\sum_r S^+_r\) whenever the robots’ behaviours are independent under every overseer strategy. Under a command bottleneck \(S^+_{\mathcal R}\) is much smaller than the sum. With randomness shared between robots it can exceed the sum.

Human power, robot power and steerability

The overseer is assumed to affect the world goals only through the robots’ joint behaviour. For world goals \(g\), let \(p_h(g)\) be the overseer’s capability, \(v_g\) the fleet’s own capability if it pursued \(g\) as a rational coalition, and \(t_g\) the largest probability with which the overseer can make the fleet behave as it would if it pursued \(g\). Human power, robot power and instructable power are

\[I_h=\log_2\sum_g p_h(g)^\zeta,\qquad I_{\mathcal R}=\log_2\sum_g v_g^\zeta,\qquad L=\log_2\sum_g (v_g t_g)^\zeta ,\]

and the results below take \(\zeta'_+=\zeta\).

  • Bounds through joint steerability. If the outcome is a deterministic function of the joint behaviour with at most \(m\) behaviour cells per world goal, then for \(\zeta\ge1\) \[S^+_{\mathcal R}-\log_2 m\;\le\;I_h\;\le\;S^+_{\mathcal R}+(\zeta-1)\log_2 m .\]
  • Power implies total, not least, steerability. Under independence and \(\zeta=1\), some robot has \(S^+_r\ge I_h/n\). Human power implies no lower bound on the least steerable robot or on \(F\).
  • Veto power. The overseer’s capability to prevent world goals, measured by the prevention form, is at most the joint prevention steerability plus \(\zeta'\log_2 m\). There is no bound in the other direction, because an outcome is prevented only if all behaviours that lead to it are prevented at once.
  • Robot power bounds human power. \(I_h\le I_{\mathcal R}\), and for \(\zeta=1\) also \(I_h\le\min(I_{\mathcal R},S^+_{\mathcal R})\).
  • A triangle inequality in effective numbers of goals. For \(\zeta\ge1\) and singleton behaviour goals, \[|\mathcal B|-2^{I_h}\;\le\;\big(|\mathcal B|-2^{I_{\mathcal R}}\big)+\big(|\mathcal B|-2^{S^+_{\mathcal R}}\big),\] where \(\mathcal B\) is the set of joint behaviours. The corresponding inequality in bits fails.

Targeted steerability

Weighting each world goal by the robots’ capability for it restores the product rule. The targeted steerability is

\[S^{\mathrm t}=L-I_{\mathcal R}=\log_2\sum_g w_g\,t_g^\zeta\le 0,\qquad w_g\propto v_g^\zeta,\]

and it gives a triangle inequality in bits, \(I_h\ge L=I_{\mathcal R}+S^{\mathrm t}\). The uncontrolled power \(U=2^{I_{\mathcal R}}-2^{L}\) is the part of the robots’ effective number of goals that the overseer cannot direct, and \(0\le U\le|\mathcal B|-2^{S^+_{\mathcal R}}\).

The anonymous aggregate of the per-robot values \(S^{\mathrm t}_r=L_r-I_r\) equals the non-anonymous aggregate above with \(\kappa'=1\) and \(S_r\) replaced by \(L_r\). A robot whose behaviour does not affect the goals has \(S^{\mathrm t}_r=0\) and does not lower this fleet value, however unsteerable it is.

Use as a constraint on an AI system

The note ends with an assessment, without results, of the following design: an AI system that acts like a regularised assistance-game agent but must not take actions that lower the long-term aggregate human power \(L\) of Heitzig and Potham (2026) or the aggregate steerability.

  • The two constraints are not redundant. \(L\) rewards the overseer’s capability to make the robots do things but gives no floor on the least steerable robot. Prevention steerability with small \(\zeta'\) supplies that floor.
  • What the simplified metric measures. For \(\zeta'\to0\) it is the reliability of a veto channel along worst-case paths. With a working stop command every action is preventable and \(S_r\approx0\). The constraint then forbids moving to states where the command works less reliably: disabling the channel, incapacitating the overseer, self-modification, creating subagents that cannot be stopped. It does not measure how finely the overseer can direct the robot.
  • Stationarity. In a finite-horizon model \(S_r\) rises as the horizon shrinks, so “do not lower \(S_r\)” needs a stationary definition. A candidate is the discounted recursion \(\log_2 M(s)=\min_{a_r}\big[\log_2 q(s,a_r)+\gamma'\min_{s'}\log_2 M(s')\big]\), whose properties have not been worked out.
  • Regularisation is confounded with responsiveness. A stochastic policy obtains moderate prevention probabilities without responding to the overseer.
  • Certainty lowers steerability. An assistance-game agent responds to the overseer only while it is uncertain about goals. The constraint is easy to satisfy only if the policy contains a committed response to a veto, which then becomes a structural requirement that the constraint protects.
  • Granularity. Prevention goals should refer to classes of actions with similar effects and to a time window in which the overseer can react.
  • Overseer model. With a boundedly rational, goal-conditioned overseer, the myopic choice in the recursion is a behavioural assumption and no longer an approximation.
  • Feasibility. “Never lower” can be infeasible, and the two constraints can conflict. A tolerance or an ordering is needed, and it has to be decided whether “lower” compares successor states with the current state or actions with each other.
  • One overseer. A robot fully steerable by one overseer can be used against everyone else. Only the constraint on \(L\) guards against that.

Limitations

The overseer is fully rational and capabilities are undiscounted. With a boundedly rational overseer the bounds on \(I_h\) can fail. Prevention steerability does not separate the overseer’s influence from the robot’s own randomness; a version relative to what the robot would do without the overseer is not developed. The recursion is greedy, and a version in which the overseer’s action maximises the continuation value is not studied. Direct effects of the overseer on the world and aggregation over time are not treated. The targeted steerability is a ratio, so a one-bit gap counts the same for a weak and a strong robot. How one robot’s capability in a fleet depends on the assumed behaviour of the others is left open.
